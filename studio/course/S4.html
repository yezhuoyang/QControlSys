Steer light with sound

The atoms need a moving spot of light. Learn what a lens does, why sound can steer a beam, and how a laboratory measures the distance moved per change of frequency.

Make a movable tweezer

A laser supplies a beam with a well-defined optical frequency. A lens focuses the beam into a small spot. In a red-detuned trap the light frequency is below the relevant atomic transition and the atom is pulled toward the intense centre. Picture a moving bowl made of light. To move the bowl sideways you can change the angle at which the beam enters the focusing lens.

Write a grating into a crystal

Send a radio-frequency electrical tone to a transducer bonded to a transparent crystal. It launches a sound wave that periodically changes how light travels through the crystal. The pattern acts like a diffraction grating, steering part of the incident light. Changing the tone frequency changes the spacing and hence the angle. This component is an acousto-optic deflector (AOD). Two perpendicular deflectors can steer in two dimensions.

Calibrate the ruler

Brooks et al. changed the tone and fitted the centre of an imaged caesium atom. On their camera axis the coordinate decreases as frequency rises. Inspect two points and divide the change in position (micrometres) by the change in frequency (megahertz). A different lens or sign convention changes that calibration. Compare it with Bluvstein’s separated pairs and ZAC’s compiled row-and-column moves. Brooks et al., Fig. 4(a) · Measured positions · Moving pairs · ZAC replay.

What must be measured to turn a requested displacement into a frequency change?

  1. The frequency-to-position calibration for this optical path
  2. Only the atom’s internal qubit frequency
Beam angle, focal length and magnification all enter the position calibration.

What to remember

A sound wave steers a beam. A lens turns angle into position. A measured calibration connects a frequency command to a distance on this apparatus.
