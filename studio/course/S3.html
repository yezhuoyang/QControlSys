Give a fast wave a shape

A radio can carry slow changes on a rapid oscillation. A qubit drive uses the same idea. Follow the pulse shape through a memory, a mixer and an output cable.

Store the outline

A bell-shaped pulse needs small samples near its ends and larger samples at its middle. A device that plays a chosen list of samples on cue is an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG). Its memory stores the outline, its clock spaces the samples, and its converter makes the voltage. Reusing one outline saves memory when the frequency or phase changes.

Turn two knobs

A mixer multiplies signals. Pick two references a quarter-cycle apart, a cosine and a sine. Multiplying each by its own slow envelope and adding the results gives control of both amplitude and phase. The envelopes are often named I and Q. In one common convention the voltage is I cos(ωt) − Q sin(ωt). Sign conventions matter when copying a calibration.

Trace the shape on two controllers

Find the envelope buffer in the QubiC drawing and follow its output to the converter. Then inspect the archived IBM pulse in M1. Its shape parameters include a duration in sample ticks and a complex amplitude, a pair of numbers that sets both quadratures. A filter and cable can change the shape before it arrives. Cryoscope used a qubit itself to measure distortion along a control line. Inspect the IBM drive · Rol et al., Cryoscope.

For the given voltage convention, what happens when both envelopes are zero?

  1. The output drive is zero
  2. The carrier frequency doubles
Both products in the sum vanish even if the reference oscillator keeps running.

What to remember

The envelope holds the shape, the oscillator holds the phase, and the mixer combines them. The receiving chip sees the signal after the delivery line.
