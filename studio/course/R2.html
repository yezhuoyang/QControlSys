Decide which state produced a signal

Suppose each trial gives a noisy wave. Compare it with a reference, combine the evidence across samples, and decide where to draw the boundary between two answers.

Line up the phases

Think of trying to hear one note in a chord. Multiply the samples by a cosine and by a sine at the note’s frequency and sum over a window. The matching frequency contributes coherently. Other frequencies tend to cancel when the window is well chosen. This is demodulation. It gives two coordinates, often called I and Q.

Learn a boundary

Prepare 0 repeatedly, then prepare 1 repeatedly. Plot the two coordinates from each shot. Two overlapping clouds appear. A discriminator assigns a bit by asking which side of a calibrated boundary contains the point. Its mistakes are visible in the overlap. Extending the integration window can gather more signal, while relaxation during a long window can change the state being read.

Close the loop

QubiC 2.0 can make a later pulse depend on a measurement taken in the same circuit. Its first requirement is that the result label reach the sequencer before the branch needs it. In the QuantumOS feed-forward example, choose c=0 and c=1 in turn. Identify the first pulse that differs. Those two runs use supplied outcome bits. QubiC 2.0 · Follow the returned bit.

Two clouds overlap. What happens to points in the overlap?

More calibration trials reveal the overlap more precisely. They cannot uniquely assign the state of every ambiguous trial.

What to remember

Demodulation extracts a tone. Integration accumulates evidence. A calibrated discriminator turns that evidence into a bit for the controller.
