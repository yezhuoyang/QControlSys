# Measured FPGA, bus and wire behavior

## Trapped-ion electrode controller

Figure 8 of Peaks, Kaarls and Noel (2026) shows the Duke controller switching across its ±10 V range in both directions. The endpoint is the module output. The bench reconstructs channel 0 as one example. The article does not identify the measured channel, FPGA package pins, SPI clock phase relative to the scope, or an attached trap load. Its three write signals, output-pin connection to the single-pole filter and scope lead are selectable in the same schematic board scene. The PCB outline, component placement, traces, returns and probe position use display coordinates in mm. [Paper and Figure 8](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01132v1#S5.SS4) · [Open the electrode comparison](http://127.0.0.1:7432/board-validation#electrode)

The high-level call `setElectrodeCode` accepts a 16-bit integer and compiles to a 24-bit, MSB-first SPI write on the 50 MHz FPGA. The model samples SDI on falling clock edges with CS low, retains the first 24 bits, and commits the addressed data register when CS rises. The example uses the published ±10 V range with asynchronous update, a powered channel and CRC disabled. The simulation starts at midscale, writes the initial rail and waits 100 µs between transaction starts. TI’s straight-binary transfer function gives −10 V at 0x0000 and approximately +9.999695 V at 0xFFFF. U10 pins 12, 13, 14 and 1 are SCLK, SDI, CS and OUT0. A user can change the source, channel recipe, wiring or filter parameters and replay the result. The reconstructed SPI sequence starts after the host UART handoff. [Paper, control design](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.01132v1#S4) · [TI DAC81416, Sections 6.3.1 and 6.5.1](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/dac81416.pdf)

The native trace advances a slew-limited DAC pin and a separate RC output on a 100 ns sample grid. The filter uses the paper’s 48.22 kHz design cutoff. For a held input and step Δt, its gain is the rounded parts-per-million value of 1 − exp(−2πfΔt). The driver and filter work in integer microvolts. The filter rounds toward the target by at least one microvolt while a difference remains. A tick at the same time as an input arrival uses the prior input. This sampled approximation bounds the time resolution of model edges. The connector measurement cannot identify the DAC pin and filter behavior separately.

The upper panel sets the effective driver slew and a single time offset. A search on 0.01 V/µs increments matches the rising 10–90% duration and yields 2.44 V/µs. Alignment uses the rising zero crossing. The time origin and rate carry over unchanged to the lower panel. The alternative nominal prediction uses 4 V/µs for the driver, with the same filter. The paper also reports a 1.76 V/µs average across channels measured at the assembled output. That quantity describes a different point in the circuit. ## Cold-atom controller

The [board measurements bench](http://127.0.0.1:7432/board-validation) replays three experiments published by Andreas Trenkwalder, Matteo Zaccanti and Nicola Poli. The experiments share a controller architecture and have separate acquisitions. Each score compares a terminal event or output of an executable machine with the corresponding observable from the archive. The authors used Cora Z7 FPGA-SoC boards for timed control of cold-atom experiments. ## Channel voltages and the timed output bus

Figure 10 records a free-running triangular DAC output and a version that advances while the start/stop input is high. Each of the ten paired Tektronix files contains 2,500 samples on a 400 µs grid. We preserve the raw CH1 voltages in millivolts. The trigger is classified at 3 V. Each transition lies halfway between the samples that bracket it, with a half-interval timing uncertainty of 200 µs. Only CH1 from the test recordings drives the program. A grid search and least-squares fit to CH2 of the first free-running capture give a period of 400,064 µs, a start 16,120 µs after the first row, a half-amplitude of 4.59021 V and a center of 0.11862 V. This is an effective waveform calibration. Analog gain and programmed amplitude cannot be identified independently from this observation.

The reconstructed FPGA program uses the paper’s 4 µs output update interval, a 50 MHz issue clock and a signed 16-bit bipolar DAC with ±10 V rails. A rising strobe latches the pending word at the selected address. Footnote 29 specifies a 240 ns lead from data update to strobe and 500 ns pulse width for the 1 MHz bus configuration. Applying that setting to this ramp, selecting address zero and assuming rising-edge conversion are reference choices. The CH2 samples resolve 100 update intervals per row. The hardware command image, exact initial register state, strobe phase, settling, ringing and inter-pin skew are not independently measured by this capture. The program initializes a held value in a 1 µs pre-roll before the first observation.

The published v1.2 FPGA pin file supplies all 16 data pins, seven address pins, the separate strobe and the input trigger pin. The buffer-card drawing in that directory has title-block revision 1.1. Its sheets 2–4 identify U1A at the trigger input, three 74HCT541 buffers and the rack connector J17. For example D0 runs from FPGA R14 to U6 pin 2 and from U6 pin 18 to J17.b25. The strobe runs from B19 to U4 pin 2 and from U4 pin 18 to J17.b8. The logic levels on either side of the output buffer use nominal 3.3 V and 5 V supplies. Package pins and rack contacts are selectable in the wiring inspector. The acquisition files do not identify which buffer revision was installed. Pin assignments describe this published reference assembly. The physical envelope, path lengths, common ground shapes, downstream DAC-card socket, buffer latency and bus service times are schematic or assumed. Buffer enabling is fixed for this experiment. Power distribution, parasitics and analog edge shapes are outside this executable signal path. [Pin constraints](https://github.com/INO-quantum/FPGA-SoC-experiment-control/blob/94cecd4eb9d04f4ebb957f70a5783b45cbd7a51a/firmware-source/2017.4/Vivado/xdc/Cora-Z7-buffer-board-v1.2.xdc) · [Buffer and connector schematic](https://github.com/INO-quantum/FPGA-SoC-experiment-control/blob/94cecd4eb9d04f4ebb957f70a5783b45cbd7a51a/buffer-card/v1.2/FPGA_buffer_board_v1.2_scheme.pdf)

The scorer executes the last causal write visible to each scope row and the held values at gate transitions. This projection uses the ideal sample-and-hold semantics of this converter. Selecting a row in the debugger reconstructs every native update within one scope interval on either side, plus the earlier state it depends on. Each write has a stable port and picosecond origin across windows. Adjacent windows cover the entire capture. The plotted prediction comes from the terminal receive events after bus, buffer, strobe and cable execution.

For the gated scope study, the compiler derives the active intervals from the measured CH1 edges, emits timed writes for those intervals and checks the input register before each interval. It reconstructs execution for the supplied trigger history. Hardware response to an unscheduled edge inside a 400 µs scope interval would require a faster acquisition.

## FPGA timing-core conformance

The separate [firmware conformance case](http://127.0.0.1:7432/machines?boardCase=rtl-bus) executes the published `dio24_timing.v` in Icarus Verilog and compares its saved pin states with the Lean execution of the same four timestamped records. Two records carry positive words, one carries a negative word, and one suppresses output with the NOP bit. All nine changes of address, signed data and strobe agree at their exact 20 ns clock offsets relative to the first data change. QControl's `writeBus` instruction drives address and data together on the same issue edge. A 12-clock delay and a 25-clock high pulse match the timing core at the package-output registers. The harness supplies the FIFO head, receive readiness and synchronized configuration at the timing module boundaries. The cross-check excludes startup latency, vendor FIFO implementation, routing delays and analog edge shapes. With Icarus Verilog 11, run `python -B validation/board/rtl/replay.py` to regenerate and check the upstream result. ## Cable propagation

Figures 6 and 8 supply primary-board estimates of one-way delay obtained from the reflected synchronization pulse and a turnaround correction. The extractor reads row type 8 from the primary-board results. The fitted line passes through the origin and weights each calibration length equally. Its effective speed is 190.075 mm/ns. The digital coax model rounds flight time up to a picosecond and adds the machine's 1 ps minimum link delay. The score reads the elapsed time between the FPGA emission and receiving connector. The held-out lengths in metres are 10.3, 11.3, 20, 20.3, 21, 21.3, 30.3 and 31.3. The source lacks 11.3 m at 270°. Measurements at other phases for the four training lengths are preserved and excluded from the scores. The experiment tests RG-58 propagation against the board's timing estimates. It does not measure DAC timing, RF attenuation or optical propagation.

## Memory bandwidth

The Figure 4 archive contains multiple DMA configurations. We select `figure_4/data/Cora-Z7-10/20201111_c10_TX.csv`, the transmit-only experiment. Its record size is 12 bytes. The TX counter reports elapsed 50 MHz clock cycles for a burst at the FIFO. We retain all 580 valid records including slow runs. The source marks one additional four-record row “remove outlier” and the normalized file records its line number. The prefilled FIFO supplies the first four records at one per clock. Later records use a shared effective interval of 35 ns inferred from the 4,096-record calibration. The replay projects completion to the next whole clock. This gives an asymptotic rate of 342.86 decimal MB/s. The 10,000,000-record holdout averaged 342.79 MB/s on the hardware.

The debugger can request the modeled time of any one of up to ten million records and the final count. Intermediate ordinals and the on-chip observation wiring are a reconstruction. The acquisition records whole-burst counts. The largest count error was 312,505 cycles (6.25 ms) on an 8,388,608-record burst. The measured variation matters for deadlines even when the mean rate is close. DDR arbitration, individual AXI handshakes, RX traffic and host startup require their own evidence. The paper separately measures two-way traffic and interrupt/host overhead.

The bandwidth panel accepts a deadline in 50 MHz cycles from FIFO start. It compares every original counter value against the inclusive deadline. At the predicted 14,680,061-cycle finish for 8,388,608 records, two of 25 hardware repetitions are late. The larger overrun is 312,505 cycles (6.2501 ms). Each retained repetition and its elapsed time can be inspected. These counts describe this data set. They supply no bound on a future run and no location for an internal stall. ## Architecture evidence for neutral atoms

Junyi Liu and colleagues, including Xiaodi Wu, describe a RISC-V-compatible quantum-control generator in RISC-Q. Section II-C discusses optical-tweezer generation, image processing and rearrangement scheduling. The public amplitude-sweep notebook records co-simulation output. Its RFSoC example and the published architecture inform controller design, but that notebook cannot supply an oscilloscope residual. A strontium tweezer experiment reports using the Cora Z7 control family together with separate RF drivers and an AWG. QuACK independently demonstrates FPGA-based loading and rearrangement of ytterbium atoms. Its installed FPGA and cable-connected daughterboards form a different apparatus. The Cora electrical scores above apply to the Trenkwalder acquisitions.
